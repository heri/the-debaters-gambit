## STEP 1 — Understand Fairness

### S1.1 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** What is Fairness?
**Body:** Fairness means everyone plays by the same rules — nobody gets special treatment just because of who they are. When something is fair, everyone has an equal chance, no matter who they are or who they know.

---

### S1.2 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** Fairness in Action
**Body:** In your classroom, everyone has to put their hand up before speaking. That rule applies to everyone — the teacher's favourite, the quiet kid in the corner, and you. That rule makes the classroom fair.

---

### S1.3 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** There's one computer in the library. Maya used it yesterday, and today both Maya and Sam want to use it. What's the fairest thing to do?

**A)** Let Maya use it again — she was there first yesterday.
→ *wrong* — "Being first yesterday doesn't make today fair. Fairness means everyone gets an equal turn, not that whoever went first keeps going first."

**B)** Let Sam use it today, since it's his turn.
→ *correct* — "Exactly! Taking turns means everyone gets the same chance. That's fairness in action."

**C)** Let whoever is taller use it.
→ *wrong* — "Height has nothing to do with fairness. Rules need to apply to everyone the same way, not based on how you look."

---

### S1.4 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** Fairness in the Bigger World
**Body:** In sports, referees make sure no team cheats. In court, judges make sure the same laws apply to everybody. Fairness keeps the whole system working — without it, people with the most power would always win.

---

### S1.5 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** A new student says: "Why do football matches even need a referee? The teams know the rules." What's the best answer?

**A)** "Because the best team needs help winning."
→ *wrong* — "Even the best team has to earn it. A referee isn't there to help one side — they're there to make sure the rules are the same for everyone."

**B)** "Because without someone watching, teams could bend the rules, and that wouldn't be fair to the team that plays honestly."
→ *correct* — "Spot on! The referee is fairness made visible — the same rules, enforced equally, for both teams."

**C)** "Because matches would be boring without them."
→ *wrong* — "Referees aren't entertainment. Their job is to make sure neither team can cheat. That's what fairness requires."

## STEP 2 — Understand the Feeling

### S2.1 — INFO
*Argus: encouraging*
**Quote (speech bubble):** "That's SO unfair! He got to be team captain TWICE this week and I've never even been asked!"
**Argus note:** Someone is very upset. Let's think carefully about what they're actually feeling.

---

### S2.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why is this person upset?

**A)** They want to be in charge of everyone.
→ *wrong* — "They're not after power for its own sake. They just want to be treated the same as everyone else."

**B)** They feel like the rules are different for them than for someone else.
→ *correct* — "Exactly! When someone else gets the same opportunity over and over and you never do, it feels like the rules don't apply equally. That's the feeling of unfairness."

**C)** They don't like the other person.
→ *wrong* — "This isn't really about that person — it's about feeling like you're being treated as less important. That's a fairness problem, not a friendship problem."

---

### S2.3 — INFO
*Argus: encouraging*
**Quote (speech bubble):** "Every time there's something good — a prize, a special job, a school trip — it's always the same people. Never me. Like I'm invisible."
**Argus note:** This isn't one bad day. This is a pattern. How many feelings can you spot?

---

### S2.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What is this person feeling?

**A)** Only angry that they were left out once.
→ *wrong* — "They are angry — but look at the words again. 'Always the same people. Never me.' That's more than one moment."

**B)** Only sad about one missed opportunity.
→ *wrong* — "Sadness is part of it — but there's a second feeling layered underneath. What else do they feel?"

**C)** Both angry and deeply invisible — like the rules simply don't include them.
→ *correct* — "Right. There's anger at the unfairness AND sadness at feeling erased. When both pile up, the feeling is much heavier. Knowing that makes your argument more powerful."

## STEP 3 — See It in Arguments

### S3.1 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** How The Fair Play Works in Arguments
**Body:** When you use The Fair Play, you remind everyone of a rule, an agreement, or a principle that already exists — and you show that your argument simply follows from it. It makes your point feel like common sense, not just your opinion.

---

### S3.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Context:** Two groups are arguing about who gets the school hall for their show. One student says: *"The schedule has always given the drama club the main hall for end-of-year shows. That agreement has been in place since the school was founded. This year shouldn't be different."*
**Prompt:** What is this student doing?

**A)** Making up a new rule to help their side.
→ *wrong* — "They're not inventing anything — they're pointing to an existing agreement. That's exactly what The Fair Play does."

**B)** Appealing to an existing agreement to prove their point.
→ *correct* — "Yes! They used 'the agreement since the school was founded' as their evidence. The rule does the arguing for them — they don't even need to fight."

**C)** Threatening the other group.
→ *wrong* — "No threats here. Just a calm, clear appeal to what was already agreed. That's much stronger than any threat."

---

### S3.3 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** The Fair Play in a Real Debate
**Body:** In a class debate about whether older students should get longer lunch breaks, a student said: *"The school handbook says every student gets equal access to the canteen. A longer break for some students but not others breaks that rule — and we shouldn't change it without everyone agreeing."*
**Argus note:** Notice: they didn't say "I want this." They said "this is what the rules already say." The rule speaks for them.

---

### S3.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why does pointing to an existing rule make an argument stronger?

**A)** Because rules are always correct and can never be questioned.
→ *wrong* — "Rules can be wrong or outdated — that's a different and important debate. But in this moment, the rule is shared ground that everyone already accepts."

**B)** Because it takes your personal opinion out of it — the rule speaks for you.
→ *correct* — "Exactly! When you say 'the rule says X,' it's not just your opinion anymore. It's something everyone agreed to. That's very hard to argue against."

**C)** Because the other side will immediately give up.
→ *wrong* — "Appealing to rules doesn't guarantee instant victory. But it puts your argument on solid ground that's very difficult to move."

## STEP 4 — Put It Together

### S4.1 — INFO (Full Technique Demonstration)
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "I think we should let the popular kids sit at the front of assembly. They work the hardest."
**Response:** "Actually, the rule for assembly seating rotates by class — everyone gets a turn at the front. We all agreed to that at the start of term. If we change it now for some students, we're breaking the agreement everyone else has been counting on."
**Argus note:** Notice: no insults, no complaints. They acknowledged what was said, then went straight to the rule. Short and solid.

---

### S4.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "We should get rid of the homework rota and just give homework to whoever the teacher thinks needs it most."
**Prompt:** Which response uses The Fair Play correctly?

**A)** "That's a terrible idea. Everyone needs homework."
→ *wrong* — "That's just an opinion — it doesn't appeal to any rule or agreement. The Fair Play needs something shared to lean on."

**B)** "I disagree with that."
→ *wrong* — "Disagreement alone isn't an argument. The Fair Play works by pointing to a rule or agreement that already exists."

**C)** "The homework rota was agreed to make sure everyone gets the same workload. Changing it means some students get judged differently than others. That breaks the agreement."
→ *correct* — "That's The Fair Play! You pointed to the existing agreement, explained its purpose, and showed why changing it would be unfair."

---

### S4.3 — INFO (Harder Scenario)
*Argus: thinking*
**Argus note:** Here's a trickier one. The opponent is ALSO arguing for fairness. How do you use The Fair Play when both sides claim it?
**Opponent says:** "The school should cancel the art show this year — not everyone has had equal time to prepare, so it's not fair to judge everyone the same way."

---

### S4.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What's the best response using The Fair Play?

**A)** "Art show is a tradition. We're having it."
→ *wrong* — "Tradition alone isn't fairness — it's just 'we've always done it.' The Fair Play needs a rule or agreement, not a habit."

**B)** "Students were told at the start of the year that the art show would happen. They've prepared based on that promise. Cancelling now is unfair to everyone who kept their side of the agreement."
→ *correct* — "Sharp! You found the fairness argument on your side — the agreement made at the start of the year. Both sides wanted fairness, but yours is anchored in a real, existing promise."

**C)** "The art show is fun for everyone, so we should keep it."
→ *wrong* — "That's appealing to shared enjoyment — that's closer to The Team. The Fair Play needs a specific rule or agreement, not just a reason it's good for everyone."

*Argus: encouraging*
**Heading:** The Fair Play — Out Loud
**Body:**
> "We all agreed at the start of term that everyone takes turns with the equipment. That rule isn't there by accident — it's there so nobody gets left out. My argument is simple: follow the agreement. That's what fairness looks like."

**Argus caption:** Read this to yourself first. Notice the structure: name the agreement → explain why it exists → state your conclusion like it's obvious. Because it is.
