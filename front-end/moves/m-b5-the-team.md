## STEP 1 — Understand the Power of Everyone Winning

### S1.1 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** What Does "Everyone Wins" Mean?
**Body:** The strongest arguments aren't ones where one side wins and the other loses. They're the ones where nobody loses. When you can show that your idea helps everyone — not just yourself — it becomes very hard to say no.

---

### S1.2 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** When Everyone Benefits
**Body:** The class is planning their end-of-year trip. One group wants a science museum. Another wants an art gallery. A third student says: *"What about the natural history museum? The science group gets fossils and evolution. The art group gets the incredible sculptures and taxidermy displays. And the school gets an educational trip that covers both."* Nobody loses.

---

### S1.3 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why is the natural history museum a strong argument in this situation?

**A)** Because it's the most popular choice.
→ *wrong* — "We don't know it's the most popular. What we know is something more important: it gives something to everyone who was arguing."

**B)** Because it gives something to each group — so nobody can reasonably say no.
→ *correct* — "Exactly! When your argument helps everyone, the people arguing against each other have no reason to say no. The Team works because it makes rejection feel unreasonable."

**C)** Because science is more important than art.
→ *wrong* — "That's a different argument entirely — and one that would just restart the fight. The Team doesn't pick a winner. It makes everyone one."

---

### S1.4 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** The Team in the Bigger World
**Body:** When a city builds a new park, a good proposal doesn't just say "people like parks." It says: "Families get a safe place for children to play. Elderly residents get a quiet place to walk. Local businesses get more foot traffic. The whole community wins." That's The Team at full scale.

---

### S1.5 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why does listing who benefits make an argument more persuasive?

**A)** Because longer lists sound more impressive.
→ *wrong* — "A long list of weak beneficiaries is still weak. What matters is that EVERY major group is genuinely served — not just mentioned."

**B)** Because it's harder to oppose a plan when you're one of the people it helps.
→ *correct* — "Right! If you're in the list of people who benefit, you'd have to argue against your own interests to say no. The Team puts everyone on the same side."

**C)** Because it avoids having to explain the actual idea.
→ *wrong* — "The Team doesn't hide the idea — it frames the idea's impact. You still have to be clear about what you're proposing."

## STEP 2 — Understand the Feeling of Being Left Out

### S2.1 — INFO
*Argus: encouraging*
**Quote (speech bubble):** "How come they get a brand new playground and our side of the school still has the old broken equipment? We're in the same school!"
**Argus note:** Someone feels like they've been forgotten. Let's look carefully at what that actually feels like from the inside.

---

### S2.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What is this person feeling?

**A)** Jealous of the other group's equipment.
→ *wrong* — "Jealousy might be part of it — but look at the last four words: 'We're in the same school.' That's the real feeling underneath."

**B)** Left out and like they're being treated as less important than others in the same community.
→ *correct* — "Exactly. This isn't pure jealousy — it's the feeling that they don't count as much as the other group. When your team isn't mentioned, you feel invisible. That's why The Team matters."

**C)** Angry that the other group got lucky.
→ *wrong* — "Luck would be random — and that would almost be easier to accept. What's hurting them is that the decision was made and they weren't included in it."

---

### S2.3 — INFO
*Argus: encouraging*
**Quote (speech bubble):** "I don't mind you having something nice. I just want to feel like we matter too. Like we're part of the same school, not a different one."
**Argus note:** This person isn't being selfish. Look at what they're actually asking for. What are both feelings here?

---

### S2.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What is this person really feeling?

**A)** Only sad that they don't have nice equipment.
→ *wrong* — "Look again: 'I don't mind you having something nice.' They're not complaining about the other group getting something. Something deeper is happening."

**B)** Only angry at the people who made the decision.
→ *wrong* — "Anger is there — but it's quiet anger, not explosive anger. The sadder feeling is in the last sentence: 'like we're part of the same school.'"

**C)** Both hurt AND feeling like they don't belong to the same community as everyone else.
→ *correct* — "Yes. There's hurt from being left out AND a deeper sadness about not feeling like a valued part of the whole. The Team directly answers both: when everyone is included, nobody has to feel this way."

## STEP 3 — See It in Arguments

### S3.1 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** How The Team Works in Arguments
**Body:** When you use The Team, you don't just argue for yourself. You name every group who benefits from your position and show exactly how. The goal is for everyone listening to hear their own name in your argument — and to realise they'd be saying no to themselves.

---

### S3.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Context:** In a class vote about adding a Friday afternoon free reading period, a student said: *"This helps the readers — they get more time with books. It helps the kids who find school tiring — it's a quiet, low-pressure end to the week. And it helps the teachers — a calm Friday afternoon instead of a difficult one. Everyone gains something."*
**Prompt:** What made this argument effective?

**A)** The student was very enthusiastic about reading.
→ *wrong* — "Personal enthusiasm is fine — but it only persuades people who share your enthusiasm. This argument was designed to reach people who might NOT care about reading."

**B)** The student identified different groups and showed each one a specific benefit.
→ *correct* — "Exactly! Readers, tired kids, teachers — three different groups, three different benefits. Everyone in the room heard themselves named. That's The Team."

**C)** The student spoke for a long time.
→ *wrong* — "Length isn't the point. This argument is actually quite short. The power comes from covering every group — not from talking more."

---

### S3.3 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** The Team in a Real Debate
**Body:** In a school council debate about starting school 15 minutes later, a student said: *"Later start helps the students who travel far — they stop arriving stressed. It helps the teachers — a calm arrival is a better start to a lesson. It helps parents — less rushing at home. And it helps the school's performance, because tired students learn less. This change costs nothing and helps everyone."*
**Argus note:** Notice the structure: group → their specific benefit → repeat. The argument is almost a list. And lists, when everyone is on them, are very hard to argue against.

---

### S3.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why does naming specific groups make The Team stronger than just saying "this is good for everyone"?

**A)** Because it makes the argument longer.
→ *wrong* — "Length isn't strength. But specificity is."

**B)** Because vague claims like 'everyone benefits' are easy to dismiss — specific groups make it real and checkable.
→ *correct* — "Right! 'Everyone benefits' sounds like an empty claim. 'This helps Year 4 students, the dinner staff, and the PE department' sounds like someone who has actually thought it through."

**C)** Because it shows you've done a lot of research.
→ *wrong* — "The groups don't have to come from research — they come from thinking carefully about who is affected. That's a skill, not a library trip."

## STEP 4 — Put It Together

### S4.1 — INFO (Full Technique Demonstration)
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "We don't need a homework club after school. Kids just want to go home."
**Response:** "Let's think about who a homework club actually helps. It helps the kids who don't have a quiet space at home — they get somewhere to concentrate. It helps the ones who get stuck and need support — they get a teacher nearby. It helps parents who work late — they don't have to worry. And it helps teachers — assignments actually get done. Nobody has to go. But lots of people need it to exist."
**Argus note:** The key phrase: "Nobody has to go." The Team never forces anyone. It just shows that removing something hurts specific people who need it.

---

### S4.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "We shouldn't bother with a school vegetable garden. It takes up space and it's extra work for the caretaker."
**Prompt:** Which response uses The Team correctly?

**A)** "I really want a garden and I think it would be fun."
→ *wrong* — "That's arguing for yourself. The Team is specifically about showing the benefit isn't just yours."

**B)** "A garden would be good for the school."
→ *wrong* — "Too vague. The Team needs to name specific groups and specific benefits. 'Good for the school' doesn't help anyone see themselves in the argument."

**C)** "The science class gets a living lab. The food technology class gets real ingredients. The caretaker gets a space managed by students, not dumped on him. And every kid who tends a plant gets to feel like they made something. Nobody loses here."
→ *correct* — "That's The Team! Science class, food tech, caretaker, every student — specific groups, specific benefits. The objection about the caretaker's workload is turned into an argument FOR the garden."

---

### S4.3 — INFO (Harder Scenario)
*Argus: thinking*
**Argus note:** This one is harder. The opponent's argument is specifically about fairness to one group. The Team needs to show that fairness to everyone — including that group — supports your side.
**Opponent says:** "Giving Year 6 a separate lunch sitting is unfair to the younger years. It feels like Year 6 get special treatment."

---

### S4.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What's the best response using The Team?

**A)** "Year 6 deserve it because they're the oldest."
→ *wrong* — "That's an argument for one group only — it actually confirms the opponent's worry that it's special treatment."

**B)** "The separate sitting helps Year 6 prepare for secondary school — they get more independence. But it also helps the younger years — the hall is less crowded and noisier for them when Year 6 isn't there. It's not special treatment. Both groups win."
→ *correct* — "That's The Team! You took the opponent's specific concern (younger years being left out) and turned it into an argument: the younger years ALSO benefit. Nobody loses."

**C)** "We can all take turns having a special sitting."
→ *wrong* — "That's a compromise — not The Team. The Team shows the current proposal already helps everyone, without needing to change it."
