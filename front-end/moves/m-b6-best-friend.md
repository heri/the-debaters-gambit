## STEP 1 — Understand Calm Certainty

### S1.1 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** What Is Calm Certainty?
**Body:** Some arguments are won not by the person who shouts loudest, but by the person who stays calmest. Calm certainty means you know what you believe, you know why you believe it — and no amount of pressure will change that. You're not stubborn. You're steady.

---

### S1.2 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** What Steady Looks Like
**Body:** Woody never changes who he is, no matter what happens. Lotso tries to convince him. Buzz turns against him. He ends up in the bin. And still, when it matters most, he knows exactly what he stands for: *"I am Woody, and I am Andy's toy."* Nothing moves him off that.

---

### S1.3 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What makes Woody's certainty convincing rather than just annoying?

**A)** He shouts louder than everyone else.
→ *wrong* — "Woody is actually quite calm. It's the steadiness, not the volume, that makes people take him seriously."

**B)** He knows his reason — and he never loses sight of it, even under extreme pressure.
→ *correct* — "Exactly. He doesn't just say 'I'm right.' He knows WHY he's right — and that knowledge is what keeps him steady. The Best Friend isn't stubbornness. It's rootedness."

**C)** He never listens to what the other side says.
→ *wrong* — "He listens — 'I hear you' is literally part of the move. What he doesn't do is let listening change his core conviction."

---

### S1.4 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** Steady Under Pressure in the Real World
**Body:** Scientists sometimes hold conclusions that most people disagree with — for years — until the evidence finally proves them right. They don't shout. They don't give up. They keep saying: "Here is my evidence. Here is why I believe it." Calmly. Again and again. That's The Best Friend at its most powerful.

---

### S1.5 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why does calm repetition work better than getting louder when someone pressures you?

**A)** Because it makes the other person feel bad for pushing.
→ *wrong* — "Making the other person feel bad is not the goal. The goal is to show that your position is solid — not to win by making them uncomfortable."

**B)** Because staying calm shows your belief is based on something real, not just emotion.
→ *correct* — "Right. If you shout or get flustered, it looks like your argument is weak and you're compensating. If you stay calm, it looks like you have nothing to fear — because you've thought it through."

**C)** Because people will eventually give up trying.
→ *wrong* — "The goal isn't to outlast people through boredom. It's to stay grounded because you're genuinely convinced. There's a big difference between those two things."

### S2.1 — INFO
*Argus: encouraging*
**Quote (speech bubble):** "But EVERYONE says you're wrong! How can you STILL think that? Maybe you're the problem."
**Argus note:** Someone is being pushed very hard to change their mind. What is the person being pressured supposed to do — and what does that pressure actually feel like?

---

### S2.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** When someone says "everyone disagrees with you," what are they really trying to do?

**A)** Help you understand that you've made a mistake.
→ *wrong* — "Maybe — but notice how they said it. 'Everyone says you're wrong' is pressure, not information. It doesn't give you any new evidence."

**B)** Use social pressure to make you doubt yourself, even without any new argument.
→ *correct* — "Exactly. 'Everyone thinks so' is not a reason — it's a tactic. It tries to make you feel isolated and wrong without actually proving anything. The Best Friend recognises this and doesn't move."

**C)** Ask you to think more carefully about your position.
→ *wrong* — "They're not asking you to think — they're telling you you're wrong because others say so. Those are very different things."

---

### S2.3 — INFO
*Argus: encouraging*
**Quote (speech bubble):** "You've been saying the same thing for ten minutes. Don't you ever change your mind? Maybe you're just not smart enough to see you're wrong."
**Argus note:** Now they're attacking the person directly, not just the argument. This is when The Best Friend is most important — and most difficult. What should happen next?

---

### S2.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What is the right move when someone attacks you personally instead of your argument?

**A)** Get angry and attack back.
→ *wrong* — "That's exactly what the pressure tactic wants — it's trying to knock you off your steady ground. Anger looks like a hit landed. Stay calm."

**B)** Apologise and change your position.
→ *wrong* — "Changing your mind because someone called you names isn't open-mindedness — it's giving in to pressure. Real open-mindedness changes for evidence and reasons, not insults."

**C)** Stay calm, acknowledge what was said, and restate your position — the attack hasn't changed your evidence.
→ *correct* — "Right. 'I hear you. And I still believe [your position], because [your reason].' The attack is irrelevant. Your argument stands on its own. The Best Friend doesn't need to defend itself — just repeat itself, calmly."

## STEP 3 — See It in Arguments

### S3.1 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** How The Best Friend Works in Arguments
**Body:** The Best Friend has one job: to hold the line. When the other side pushes back, you don't get defensive, you don't raise your voice, you don't add ten new arguments. You simply acknowledge what they said — and then restate your position, with the same calm certainty as the first time.

---

### S3.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Context:** In a debate about whether kids should choose their own bedtime, the opponent pushed back three times. Each time, the student replied: *"I hear you — and I still believe kids should have a say in their own bedtime, because they know better than anyone how tired they actually are."*
**Prompt:** Why does this response work even when repeated?

**A)** Because repetition is confusing.
→ *wrong* — "Repetition isn't supposed to confuse — it's supposed to demonstrate that the position hasn't changed, and there's no pressure that can change it."

**B)** Because each time it's repeated, it shows the position is grounded — not a guess or a fleeting opinion.
→ *correct* — "Yes! Every time you calmly repeat the same thing, it says: 'This isn't a bluff. This isn't a reaction. This is what I actually believe.' That's very hard to break down."

**C)** Because eventually the opponent will run out of things to say.
→ *wrong* — "The goal isn't to outlast — it's to stay grounded. You're repeating because you're sure, not because you're waiting them out."

---

### S3.3 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** The Best Friend in a Real Debate
**Body:** In a student council speech, a candidate was challenged four times by an audience member who kept saying their plan "would never work." Each time, the candidate said: *"I understand the concern. And I still believe it's the right plan, because the evidence from schools that have tried it shows it works. That hasn't changed."*
**Argus note:** Notice: they acknowledged the challenge every single time. They never ignored it. But they also never moved. The acknowledgement without movement is the whole technique.

---

### S3.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why is it important to acknowledge what the other side says, even when you're not going to change your position?

**A)** To make them feel heard so they'll stop pushing.
→ *wrong* — "That's a side effect, not the reason. You acknowledge because you ARE listening — you just disagree. There's a difference between not listening and not agreeing."

**B)** Because ignoring their point looks like you can't answer it — acknowledgement shows you've heard and considered it.
→ *correct* — "Exactly. 'I hear you' isn't just politeness — it's proof that you engaged with what they said. And then moving back to your position shows you considered it and weren't moved. That's more powerful than pretending they didn't speak."

**C)** Because the rules of debate say you have to.
→ *wrong* — "This isn't about rules — it's about what works. Acknowledgement without movement is a very specific technique that makes you look thoughtful AND unshakeable."

## STEP 4 — Put It Together

### S4.1 — INFO (Full Technique Demonstration)
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "You're wrong about dogs being better than cats. Everyone knows cats are more independent and easier to look after."
**Response:** "I hear you — cats are definitely more independent, and that's a real advantage. And I still believe dogs are better companions for most families, because the loyalty and affection dogs give is something most people want from a pet more than independence."
**Argus note:** Notice: three moves. First: 'I hear you.' Second: find something true in their point and say so. Third: 'And I still believe…' followed by your reason. Calm, grounded, unshakeable.

---

### S4.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "Pizza is obviously NOT a good breakfast food. It has no nutritional value and you know it. You're just saying it because you like pizza."
**Prompt:** Which response uses The Best Friend correctly?

**A)** "That's completely wrong! Pizza has tomatoes and cheese which are nutritious!"
→ *wrong* — "That's getting pulled into an argument about nutrition — which is getting defensive. The Best Friend doesn't scramble to find new evidence. It holds the line it already had."

**B)** "You might be right that it's not the healthiest option. I still believe pizza can be a valid breakfast because breakfast is about starting the day with something you enjoy — and enjoyment matters."
→ *correct* — "Perfect! 'You might be right' acknowledges their point. 'I still believe' holds the line. The reason is the same as it always was. Nothing moved. That's The Best Friend."

**C)** "Fine. Maybe pizza isn't that good for breakfast."
→ *wrong* — "That's capitulating to pressure — not changing because of a new argument. The Best Friend only moves for genuine new reasons, not because someone pushed harder."

---

### S4.3 — INFO (Harder Scenario)
*Argus: thinking*
**Argus note:** The hardest version of The Best Friend is when the opponent makes a point that's genuinely quite good. Do you acknowledge it — or do you hold your ground? The answer: both.
**Opponent says:** "I understand you want kids to decide their own bedtime — but kids who stay up late actually do worse in school, and that's been proven by science. You can't argue against that."

---

### S4.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What's the best response using The Best Friend?

**A)** "That science is probably wrong."
→ *wrong* — "Dismissing evidence you can't counter is not The Best Friend — it's denial. The Best Friend acknowledges truth, then holds its ground anyway."

**B)** "I hear you — and that research matters. And I still believe kids should have a SAY, even if the final decision is made with parents. Input and full control are different things. That hasn't changed."
→ *correct* — "Brilliant! You acknowledged the science ('that research matters'). You then held your position by narrowing it to something still defensible — not full control, but having a say. The Best Friend adjusts the frame only when necessary, never the core conviction."

**C)** "I never said kids should stay up really late. I just said they should choose."
→ *wrong* — "That's trying to rewrite what you said, not stand by it. The Best Friend holds what it always believed — it doesn't retreat by pretending it said something slightly different."
