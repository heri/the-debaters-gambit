## STEP 1 — Understand Honest Courage

### S1.1 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** What is Honest Courage?
**Body:** Brave doesn't always mean fighting. Sometimes the bravest thing you can do is admit that something is true — even when it makes your position look harder to defend. And then explain why you're still right anyway. That takes more courage than pretending the flaw doesn't exist.

---

### S1.2 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** Simba's Courage
**Body:** Simba ran away from the Pride Lands and hid for years. When Nala found him, he didn't pretend that never happened. He said: *"Yes — I ran. And I'm coming back."* He admitted the weakness, and then showed why it didn't change what he was about to do. That's The Lion's Courage.

---

### S1.3 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why does admitting a flaw actually make Simba MORE convincing, not less?

**A)** Because it makes people feel sorry for him.
→ *wrong* — "Pity isn't the goal — trust is. Simba doesn't want people to feel sorry for him. He wants them to believe in what he's about to do."

**B)** Because admitting the truth shows he isn't hiding anything — so when he says 'I'm coming back,' it sounds real.
→ *correct* — "Exactly. If he pretended he'd never run away, everyone would know he was lying — and nothing else he said would be trustworthy. By admitting the flaw, he earns the right to be believed."

**C)** Because it gets the embarrassing part out of the way early.
→ *wrong* — "That's one way to think about it — but the deeper point is that honesty builds credibility. Once you've admitted the hard thing, everything else you say sounds more real."

---

### S1.4 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** Honest Courage in the Real World
**Body:** When a city council proposed a new bus route, a councillor said: *"Yes, it will mean closing three stops in the village. That's a real inconvenience — I won't pretend otherwise. But the new route will carry four times as many passengers and reduce the journey time for everyone else by 20 minutes. The trade-off is worth it."*

---

### S1.5 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why does admitting the bus stop closure make the argument stronger?

**A)** Because it makes the opponents feel heard.
→ *wrong* — "That's a side effect, not the mechanism. The real reason is more specific — about credibility."

**B)** Because pretending the closures don't exist would immediately destroy the argument's credibility.
→ *correct* — "Right! Everyone in that room knows three stops are closing. If the speaker pretended otherwise, nobody would trust anything else they said. Admitting it first is the only move that keeps them in the room."

**C)** Because it proves the proposal is balanced.
→ *wrong* — "Admitting a downside doesn't automatically prove balance — it just proves honesty. The argument still has to show the benefit outweighs the cost."

## STEP 2 — Understand the Feeling of Being Caught

### S2.1 — INFO
*Argus: encouraging*
**Quote (speech bubble):** "You said ice cream for breakfast would make kids smarter — but sugar is really bad for you. Doesn't that prove you're completely wrong?"
**Argus note:** Someone has just found a weakness in an argument. What is the person on the other side feeling right now — and what should they do?

---

### S2.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** When someone points out a genuine weakness in your argument, what is the worst thing you can do?

**A)** Admit it and explain why you're still right.
→ *wrong* — "That's actually The Lion's Courage — it's the RIGHT move, not the worst one."

**B)** Pretend the weakness doesn't exist or change the subject.
→ *correct* — "Exactly. Pretending a real flaw isn't there is the worst move — everyone can see it, and now they also know you're not being honest. You've lost both the argument and the trust."

**C)** Get frustrated and repeat your original point louder.
→ *wrong* — "Getting louder when you've been caught doesn't help — it just confirms that you don't have a real answer. But it's not quite as bad as pretending the flaw doesn't exist, because at least you're still engaging."

---

### S2.3 — INFO
*Argus: encouraging*
**Quote (speech bubble):** "You see? You CAN'T answer that, can you? That means I've won. You should just give up now."
**Argus note:** They think they've won because they found a weakness. But finding a weakness doesn't automatically win the argument — unless the other side lets it. What happens next matters enormously.

---

### S2.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** When an opponent says "you can't answer that," what does The Lion's Courage say to do?

**A)** Agree that they've made a strong point and give up.
→ *wrong* — "A strong point is not the same as a winning argument. The Lion's Courage agrees the point is true — then shows why it doesn't decide the whole question."

**B)** Deny the weakness exists.
→ *wrong* — "Denial only works if the weakness isn't real. If it is real, denial destroys credibility. The Lion's Courage is honest precisely because honesty is the strongest move here."

**C)** Acknowledge the weakness directly and then show why your argument still stands.
→ *correct* — "Right! 'Yes, it's true that [weakness]. AND here's why that doesn't decide the question.' Admitting it takes the wind out of their 'you can't answer that' — because you just did."

## STEP 3 — See It in Arguments

### S3.1 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** How The Lion's Courage Works in Arguments
**Body:** The Lion's Courage has a very specific structure: admit the flaw → then pivot with 'but' or 'and yet' → then explain why your argument still holds. The admission does two things: it shows you're honest, and it shows you've already thought about the hard part.

---

### S3.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Context:** In a debate about whether cats would make better spies than dogs, a student said: *"Yes, it's true that cats are completely unpredictable and might just sit down in the middle of a mission. BUT — that unpredictability is exactly what makes them impossible to track. No spy agency would ever expect the asset that stops to nap. That's perfect cover."*
**Prompt:** What did this student do?

**A)** Changed the subject when things got difficult.
→ *wrong* — "They didn't change the subject — they went straight into the flaw. The whole point is that they faced it head-on."

**B)** Admitted a genuine weakness and then turned it into a strength.
→ *correct* — "Exactly! They didn't just admit the flaw — they flipped it. The unpredictability they admitted became part of the argument FOR cats. That's the most advanced version of The Lion's Courage."

**C)** Agreed with the opponent.
→ *wrong* — "They agreed with one specific point — the unpredictability. But they didn't agree with the conclusion. Agreeing with a fact is not the same as surrendering the argument."

---

### S3.3 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** The Lion's Courage in a Real Debate
**Body:** In a class debate about whether video games should count as homework, a student said: *"Yes, it's true that some games are a complete waste of time. I won't pretend every game teaches something useful. But Minecraft genuinely teaches spatial reasoning and planning. Kerbal Space Program teaches real physics. The category includes good and bad — and dismissing all of it because of the bad ones is like banning all books because some books are rubbish."*
**Argus note:** Notice: they gave up the weakest part of their argument before their opponent could use it against them. That left the strongest part standing alone.

---

### S3.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why is it strategic to admit your weakness before your opponent points it out?

**A)** Because it takes away their best attack.
→ *correct* — "Yes! If you admit the flaw first, your opponent can't use it against you — you've already dealt with it. And the fact that you brought it up yourself makes you look more trustworthy."

**B)** Because it proves your argument is weak.
→ *wrong* — "Admitting one flaw doesn't prove the whole argument is weak — it proves the rest of it is solid enough to survive honesty. That's actually a strength."

**C)** Because judges reward honesty.
→ *wrong* — "Maybe — but that's not why The Lion's Courage works. It works because admitting a real flaw and surviving it is more persuasive than pretending flaws don't exist."

## STEP 4 — Put It Together

### S4.1 — INFO (Full Technique Demonstration)
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "Your idea of having a class pet is irresponsible. Pets need constant care and kids will forget."
**Response:** "Yes, it's true that kids forget things — I won't pretend that's not a real risk. But here's why that doesn't decide the question: forgetting is exactly why we need the responsibility in the first place. The cage rota, the feeding chart, the reminder system — those ARE the lesson. You don't learn responsibility by being given things you can't forget."
**Argus note:** Notice the structure exactly: 'Yes, it's true that [flaw].' → 'But here's why that doesn't decide the question.' → Explanation. The flaw becomes part of the argument FOR, not against.

---

### S4.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "Kids can't manage their own bedtime. They'll just stay up all night playing games."
**Prompt:** Which response uses The Lion's Courage correctly?

**A)** "That's not true. Kids are more responsible than adults give them credit for."
→ *wrong* — "That's a denial — and not a very convincing one. Some kids WOULD stay up too late. Pretending that's never true loses the audience who know it sometimes is."

**B)** "Yes, it's true that some kids would push it too far at first. AND that's exactly what a trial period is for — two weeks with a chosen bedtime, and if the morning routine suffers, the rule comes back. The risk is real, and it's manageable."
→ *correct* — "That's The Lion's Courage! You admitted the real risk ('some kids would push it'), then you turned that admission into the reason for a structured trial. The flaw becomes the argument."

**C)** "I hear you — and I still believe kids should decide their own bedtime."
→ *wrong* — "That's The Best Friend — a different move. The Lion's Courage specifically admits the flaw before holding its ground."

---

### S4.3 — INFO (Harder Scenario)
*Argus: thinking*
**Argus note:** This one is harder — the opponent's flaw is a real and important one. The Lion's Courage has to work harder to show why it still doesn't decide the question.
**Opponent says:** "Library books go missing all the time because nobody takes care of them. Your argument that libraries are more powerful than the internet falls apart when the books are gone."

---

### S4.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What's the best response using The Lion's Courage?

**A)** "Libraries are well-maintained. That's not a real problem."
→ *wrong* — "Books DO go missing — that's a real thing that happens. Denying it doesn't make it go away and damages credibility."

**B)** "Yes, it's true that books go missing — and that's a genuine problem. But it's a solvable one: better systems, library cards, return reminders. The internet has problems too — misinformation, broken links, paywalls. Neither system is perfect. The question is which one serves readers better overall, and I still believe libraries do."
→ *correct* — "Sharp! You admitted the flaw fully, showed it's fixable, then did the same thing to the other side, and brought the debate back to the real question. That's The Lion's Courage at full strength."

**C)** "The internet has even more problems than libraries."
→ *wrong* — "That's deflection — pointing at the other side's problems instead of acknowledging your own. The Lion's Courage specifically starts with your OWN flaw."
