## STEP 1 — Understand the Bigger Purpose

### S1.1 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** What's It Really About?
**Body:** Sometimes people argue about something small — but underneath it, there's a much bigger reason why it matters. The Golden Rule asks you to pause and ask: what does everyone here actually want? What is this really about?

---

### S1.2 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** Finding the Bigger Goal
**Body:** Mia and her brother are fighting about who gets the TV remote. On the surface, it looks like an argument about a remote control. But underneath? They've both had a long day and they both just want to relax. That's what the argument is really about.

---

### S1.3 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Mia and her brother are arguing about the TV. What are they REALLY arguing about?

**A)** Which shows are better.
→ *wrong* — "That's the surface — the thing they're fighting over. But look underneath: why does each of them want the TV at all?"

**B)** They both want to rest and unwind after a long day.
→ *correct* — "Exactly! The TV is just the surface. Underneath, they both want the same thing: a break. Once you see that, you can find an answer that works for both of them."

**C)** Who has more power at home.
→ *wrong* — "Power might creep in — but that's not the core need here. They're both just tired. Start with the simplest explanation."

---

### S1.4 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** A Bigger Stage
**Body:** In a town debate, some people want to build a new road. Others are angry because it would go through a park they love. On the surface, it looks like they want opposite things. But they both actually want the same thing: a better, happier community for everyone who lives there.

---

### S1.5 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** In the road vs. park debate, what do both sides REALLY want?

**A)** Specifically a road, or specifically a park — one or the other.
→ *wrong* — "Those are what they're each asking for — but they're the means, not the end. What goal are both trying to reach?"

**B)** To win the argument so the other side has to give up.
→ *wrong* — "Winning might feel satisfying in the moment — but it's not the underlying need. People argue because something they care about is at stake."

**C)** A better life for their community — they just disagree on how to get there.
→ *correct* — "Right! Both sides want the same destination. The Golden Rule finds that shared destination and argues that your path is the best way to reach it."

## STEP 3 — See It in Arguments

### S3.1 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** How The Golden Rule Works in Arguments
**Body:** When you use The Golden Rule, you pause the surface argument and ask out loud: what does everyone here actually want? Then you show that your answer is the one that gets everyone there. It's very hard to argue against your own goal.

---

### S3.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Context:** In a debate about homework, a student said: *"We're not really arguing about homework. We're arguing about how kids learn best. If that's the real goal, then homework that feels connected to real life will work better than homework that feels like a punishment."*
**Prompt:** What did this student do?

**A)** Changed the subject to avoid the question.
→ *wrong* — "They didn't change the subject — they found the REAL subject underneath the one everyone was arguing about. That's the whole move."

**B)** Found the shared goal (learning well) and argued their answer reaches it.
→ *correct* — "Yes! They lifted the debate above 'homework yes or no' to 'what helps kids learn?' — and then answered that question. That's The Golden Rule."

**C)** Agreed with the other side to end the argument.
→ *wrong* — "They didn't agree — they reframed the entire debate. That's not giving in. That's taking control of what the debate is actually about."

---

### S3.3 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** The Golden Rule in a Bigger Debate
**Body:** In a student council debate about extending lunch break, a student said: *"Let's think about what lunch break is actually for. It's not just about eating — it's about recharging so we can learn better in the afternoon. If that's the goal, then a slightly longer break doesn't cost us time. It buys us focus."*
**Argus note:** They didn't argue about lunch. They argued about learning. Same conclusion — completely different ground. That's the power of The Golden Rule.

---

### S3.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why is it powerful to reframe an argument around the deeper goal?

**A)** Because it makes your opponent look like they're missing the point.
→ *wrong* — "The Golden Rule isn't about making your opponent look bad. It's about finding the thing everyone already agrees on — and building from there."

**B)** Because it shifts the debate to ground that both sides already care about.
→ *correct* — "Exactly! When you name the shared goal, you're not fighting anymore. You're showing how your answer reaches a destination everyone wants. That's much harder to argue against."

**C)** Because it confuses the other side and they make mistakes.
→ *wrong* — "Confusion isn't the goal — clarity is. The Golden Rule wins by making your argument feel obvious, not bewildering."

## STEP 4 — Put It Together

### S4.1 — INFO (Full Technique Demonstration)
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "We should cancel the book club. Nobody's that interested anymore."
**Response:** "Before we decide, let's think about what book club is actually for. It's not just about reading — it's about finding kids who love stories and giving them somewhere to belong. If that's the goal, the question isn't 'is it popular?' It's 'are there kids who need it?' And the answer to that is yes."
**Argus note:** Notice: they stepped BACK from the surface question (is it popular?) and asked what the thing is really FOR. Then they answered that deeper question. That's The Golden Rule.

---

### S4.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "We shouldn't get a class pet. It's too much work and someone always ends up doing all the cleaning."
**Prompt:** Which response uses The Golden Rule correctly?

**A)** "Pets are not that much work. Stop exaggerating."
→ *wrong* — "That argues the surface complaint (too much work) without asking what the class pet is really for. The Golden Rule asks the deeper question first."

**B)** "I love animals and I'll do all the cleaning myself."
→ *wrong* — "That's a kind offer — but it's still stuck on the surface problem. The Golden Rule finds the shared goal, not just a solution to one complaint."

**C)** "The real question is whether a class pet helps us learn to care for something living. If that's what we're here to find out, then the work is part of the lesson."
→ *correct* — "That's The Golden Rule! You lifted the debate above 'too much work' and argued for the deeper purpose — learning responsibility. Now the work becomes an argument FOR the pet, not against it."

---

### S4.3 — INFO (Harder Scenario)
*Argus: thinking*
**Argus note:** This one is trickier. The opponent is making a good point about fairness. How do you use The Golden Rule when the other side isn't clearly wrong?
**Opponent says:** "Screen time rules are unfair because some kids are more responsible than others. We should be trusted individually."

---

### S4.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What's the best response using The Golden Rule?

**A)** "The rules are the rules and they apply to everyone equally."
→ *wrong* — "That's The Fair Play — correct card for some situations, but not The Golden Rule. The Golden Rule asks what the rule is actually FOR."

**B)** "Screen time rules aren't really about trust — they're about making sure everyone has time for other things too. If the goal is balance, the rules help everyone reach it, whether they're 'responsible' or not."
→ *correct* — "Sharp! You found the purpose of the rule (balance, not punishment) and showed that your side serves that purpose. The opponent argued about trust; you argued about goals. The Golden Rule."

**C)** "You're right. Responsible kids should get different rules."
→ *wrong* — "That's conceding the point, not reframing it. The Golden Rule reframes the debate — it doesn't surrender the ground."
