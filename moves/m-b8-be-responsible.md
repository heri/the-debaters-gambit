## STEP 1 — Understand Responsibility

### S1.1 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** What Does Responsibility Mean?
**Body:** Being responsible means thinking not just about what YOU want, but about what's right for everyone around you. It means asking: if I get this, what happens to everyone else? And it means being willing to argue for the answer that's good for the whole group — even when it's harder.

---

### S1.2 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** Responsibility in Action
**Body:** Steven Universe could have just protected his friends and gone home. Instead, he kept asking: *"What about the people we're fighting against? Don't they deserve help too? What's the solution that doesn't leave anyone behind?"* That's what responsibility looks like — holding a wider view when everyone else has narrowed theirs.

---

### S1.3 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Your class has one big tin of biscuits. Five students want them all. The responsible thing to do is:

**A)** Give them to whoever asks first.
→ *wrong* — "Being first doesn't make something right for everyone. Responsibility means thinking about the whole group, not just rewarding speed."

**B)** Split them equally so every student in the class gets some.
→ *correct* — "Right! Responsibility means thinking beyond the five students who spoke up. The rest of the class exists too — and the right answer includes them."

**C)** Give them to the most popular student to share however they want.
→ *wrong* — "That transfers responsibility to someone else instead of solving the problem. A responsible argument takes the whole group on directly."

---

### S1.4 — INFO
*Argus: confident*
**Heading:** Responsibility in the Bigger World
**Body:** A class president who only helps their friends isn't doing their job. A good class president makes decisions that are fair for the whole class — even the students who didn't vote for them, even the students who are difficult, even the students who never say anything. That's the weight of responsibility.

---

### S1.5 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why is thinking about EVERYONE — not just the people who voted for you — the sign of a responsible leader?

**A)** Because it makes more people like you.
→ *wrong* — "Popularity might follow — but that's not the reason. A responsible leader does the right thing even when it doesn't win them any extra friends."

**B)** Because responsibility means your decisions affect everyone, so everyone deserves to be considered.
→ *correct* — "Exactly. If your decision affects someone, they deserve to be in your thinking. Leaving people out of your consideration doesn't make them disappear — it just means your decision harms them without you having to see it."

**C)** Because teachers will give you better marks for it.
→ *wrong* — "Responsibility isn't for marks or rewards. It's what you owe to the people your decisions affect."

**A)** It's against the rules.

**B)** It treats the other people as less important — as if their needs simply don't count.
→ *correct* — "Yes. Taking everything for yourself doesn't just leave others without — it sends a message: 'You don't matter to me.' That's what irresponsibility does."

**C)** It will make them angry at you.
→ *wrong* — "The consequence for YOU isn't why it's wrong. It's wrong because of what it does to THEM — their needs go unmet and they feel unseen."

## STEP 3 — See It in Arguments

### S3.1 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** How Be Responsible Works in Arguments
**Body:** When you use Be Responsible, you don't just argue for what you want. You lift your argument to the level of what's right for everyone. Instead of "this is good for me," you say: "this is good for all of us — and that's why it's the right choice." It's very hard to argue against someone who is genuinely trying to look out for everyone.

---

### S3.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Context:** In a class meeting about how to use the leftover budget, a student said: *"I know some of us want new books for the library and others want new sports equipment. I think we should split it. It's not the most exciting answer — but it's the one that doesn't leave half the class feeling forgotten. That's what's right for all of us."*
**Prompt:** What makes this a responsible argument?

**A)** It's a compromise, which is always the fairest option.
→ *wrong* — "Compromise isn't always the most responsible answer — sometimes the responsible answer is to stand firm. This argument is responsible for a different reason."

**B)** It explicitly puts the whole class's feeling of belonging above any one group's preference.
→ *correct* — "Yes! 'Doesn't leave half the class feeling forgotten' is Be Responsible in action. The argument isn't 'I want a split.' It's 'a split is what's right for all of us.'"

**C)** It avoids conflict, which makes everyone happier.
→ *wrong* — "Avoiding conflict isn't the goal of Be Responsible — serving the whole group is. Sometimes being responsible means having a harder conversation, not an easier one."

---

### S3.3 — INFO
*Argus: thinking*
**Heading:** Be Responsible in a Real Debate
**Body:** In a debate about whether homework should be abolished, a student said: *"I know a lot of us want less homework. But I think about the students who actually need the structure — whose home life is chaotic and who need school to give them something reliable. Abolishing homework helps some of us and hurts others. We need to find the answer that's fair for everyone — not just the loudest group in the room."*
**Argus note:** Notice: they argued FOR students they were not. That's the most powerful form of Be Responsible — advocating for the group that isn't speaking, because they deserve to be in the room too.

---

### S3.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** Why is arguing for people who aren't speaking one of the most powerful things you can do in a debate?

**A)** Because it makes you look generous.
→ *wrong* — "Looking generous is a side effect. The real power is in the argument itself — you're showing that your position covers more people, which makes it harder to dismiss."

**B)** Because it shows your argument applies to the whole situation — not just the people in the room.
→ *correct* — "Exactly! An argument that only covers the people shouting in the room is easy to defeat — just point at who it leaves out. An argument that includes the quiet people too is much, much harder to attack."

**C)** Because the judge will feel sorry for the people you mention.
→ *wrong* — "Sympathy isn't the mechanism. The mechanism is coverage — your argument is stronger because it reaches more people."

## STEP 4 — Put It Together

### S4.1 — INFO (Full Technique Demonstration)
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "We should only get new playground equipment for the sports kids. They use it the most, so they should get the most."
**Response:** "I understand that argument — but school resources belong to everyone, not just the students who use them most. The kids who don't play sports might just need a different kind of space — a bench, a shelter, somewhere quiet to sit with friends. If we only spend money on the loudest group, we're telling the quieter ones they don't count. That's not what's right for all of us."
**Argus note:** Notice: they didn't attack the sports kids — they just expanded the picture. Be Responsible never argues against one group. It argues for the group that was left out.

---

### S4.2 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Opponent says:** "We should let the older kids have the computers first because they have more important work to do."
**Prompt:** Which response uses Be Responsible correctly?

**A)** "The younger kids also deserve computer time just as much."
→ *wrong* — "That's a fairness argument (The Fair Play). Be Responsible needs to be grounded in what's right for the whole community — not just one group's equal claim."

**B)** "I hear what you're saying. But if we give one group priority, we're deciding that some students matter more than others. The right system is one that works fairly for everyone — because every student here deserves to be considered."
→ *correct* — "That's Be Responsible! You didn't deny the older kids' needs — you widened the frame to include everyone. 'Every student here deserves to be considered' is the core of the move."

**C)** "That would make the younger kids feel left out."
→ *wrong* — "That's The Kind Heart — focusing on feelings. Be Responsible is about the right choice for everyone, not just about managing one group's feelings."

---

### S4.3 — INFO (Harder Scenario)
*Argus: thinking*
**Argus note:** This one is harder. The opponent is also using a responsibility-style argument — arguing for a specific group who needs help. You need to show your answer takes everyone into account, including that group.
**Opponent says:** "We should let kids who have no books at home borrow the class library books and keep them longer. They need it more."

---

### S4.4 — QUESTION
*Argus: thinking*
**Prompt:** What's the best response using Be Responsible?

**A)** "Everyone should get equal time with the books. No exceptions."
→ *wrong* — "That's The Fair Play — equal rules. But Be Responsible sometimes means recognising that equal treatment of unequal situations isn't actually fair."

**B)** "I agree those students need more support — and I think the responsible answer is to get MORE books so nobody has to choose. If the budget is the problem, we solve the budget problem. We don't solve a resource problem by deciding some students matter less."
→ *correct* — "Brilliant! You agreed with the opponent's underlying concern (those students need support) AND expanded the solution so nobody loses. That's Be Responsible at its most sophisticated — taking care of everyone by solving the bigger problem."

**C)** "That's not fair to the other students."
→ *wrong* — "That's a fairness complaint — it doesn't offer a responsible solution. Be Responsible doesn't just say 'that's unfair.' It proposes the answer that's right for everyone."
